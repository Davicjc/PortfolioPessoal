A field-service management platform: technician scheduling, a real-time map with routes, chat with video calls, AI-assisted dispatch and on-site customer signatures. It runs in the browser, as a Windows app and as an Android app — with no server of its own to maintain.

The problem

At Audicom, service orders were scattered across the ISP's billing system (HubSoft), spreadsheets and WhatsApp. Managers couldn't see where each technician was, technicians got addresses by text message, and proof of a visit was a photo lost in someone's camera roll. What was missing: one system the office and the field use at the same time, on any technician's phone, without adding a server the company would have to run.

Decisions

  1. 01

    Real time without a server

    Firestore is both the data layer and the signaling channel: schedules, chat and technician locations reach every screen instantly, and voice/video calls are peer-to-peer WebRTC with the database exchanging the offers. Cloud cost stays close to zero.

  2. 02

    Read-only HubSoft, behind an allow-list

    The system reads customers, contracts, equipment and bandwidth usage from HubSoft over OAuth, but every call goes through a closed allow-list of GET routes. Routes that disconnect a customer or expose passwords are banned in code, and any password in a response is stripped before it reaches the screen.

  3. 03

    One product, three platforms

    The same web codebase ships as a PWA, a Windows app (C# with WebView2, its own installer and a self-update that keeps shortcuts intact) and an Android APK. A single version registry tracks what is outdated on each platform.

  4. 04

    AI suggests, the manager decides

    The dispatch assistant reads open orders, distances between stops and the day's crews, and proposes assignments with a plain-language explanation. Nothing changes until a manager approves — and every module, AI included, can be switched on or off per customer.
